    LEVEL 3 · MASTER'S THESIS

    How Predictable is Love?

    Machine Learning meets Partner Choice

    Linköping University · 30 ECTS · Spring 2026 Supervisor: Benjamin Jarvis · Co-supervisor: Jesper Lindmarker
    BOSS HP
    STAGE 1 · MISSION BRIEFING

    “Given a woman's sociodemographic profile and the pool of eligible men in her city, can a model identify the man she actually married?”

    Sociology knows a lot about who marries whom on average: people tend to pair up with partners like themselves (assortative mating). Much less is known about whether that pattern is strong enough to predict an individual choice. This thesis treats partner choice as a prediction problem and uses machine learning to test what the answer says about two competing theories:

    EXCHANGE THEORY

    Partners are weighed on several traits at once, and a weakness on one can be traded off against a strength on another. Preferences are smooth and compensatory.

    VS
    BOUNDARY THEORY

    Sorting is categorical. Some traits are deal-breakers that filter out whole groups before anything else counts.

    STAGE 2 · THE MAP

    American Community Survey via IPUMS USA: about 1% of the U.S. population is surveyed each year. The sample is recently married (within 3 years), co-residing couples in identifiable metro areas.

    AGE

    Young couples are usually the same age. From the late twenties the husband tends to be older, and the median gap grows to about 5–6 years for men in their 40s–50s.

    RACE

    Same-race marriage: 87.4% of White women, 85.1% of Black women, 70.3% of Hispanic women and 66.4% of Asian women.

    EDUCATION

    A strong but imperfect diagonal: 54% of high-school-graduate women married high-school-graduate men, and 46% of women with a bachelor's married men with a bachelor's.

    Each woman is paired with her real husband plus 9 decoys: men from the same metro and year she could plausibly have met.

    Only three ingredients: age gap, education gap and same race/ethnicity. Each describes the pair, not either person alone.

    3

    Score every eligible man in her metro and see where the real husband lands. 50% = random guessing, and lower is better.

    PLOT TWIST

    Race has the biggest coefficient (b = 1.65, so crossing a racial boundary weighs about as much as 7.5 extra years of age gap). Yet dropping age hurts prediction about 5× more (+13.5 pp vs +2.8 pp). The reason is variance. Race is one yes/no switch, and in many cities a same-race match happens by chance anyway. Age varies across decades, so it can sort hundreds of candidates much more finely. A big coefficient is not the same thing as predictive power.

    STAGE 7 · WORLD SELECT

    The models were re-run on every woman in four cities, before and after COVID. The spline model won 7 of 8 city-period matches.

    More diverse markets are harder to predict. Diversity doesn't weaken preferences, but same-race matches become rarer by chance, so the race signal carries less information. Atlanta (82.4% same-race marriage) is the easiest level and San Francisco (69.5%) the hardest.

    STAGE 8 · SCOREBOARD
    ✔
    H1 · SUPPORTED

    Age, education and race together beat any subset. Each adds a signal the other two can't replace.

    ◐
    H2 · PARTIAL

    The forest beats the linear model, but a well-specified spline matches or beats the forest. The forest's edge came from misspecification, not deal-breaker preferences.

    ✘
    H3 · NOT SUPPORTED

    Race was expected to matter most for prediction. In fact age is the most important predictor, both in the ablation and in the forest's own permutation importance (0.152 vs 0.073).

    Three demographic traits put the true husband in the top ~14% of every eligible man in his city.

    🧭

    ML as a diagnostic

    Use the forest to find out what a simple model misses, then rebuild it in an interpretable form instead of swapping in a black box.

    ⚖️

    Exchange > boundaries

    At population scale, smooth compensatory trade-offs describe partner choice at least as well as hard categorical filters.

    ⏳

    Life-course timing

    Age does most of the sorting work, so when people marry may matter more than the literature has assumed.

    KNOWN GLITCHES (LIMITATIONS)

    • Observational: it predicts who she married, not who she would have preferred.
    • Only age, education and race. Personality, values and attraction aren't in the data.
    • Married, co-residing couples only, modelled from the woman's side.

    MINI-BOSS BA THESIS · RADBOUD UNIVERSITY · JUNE 2024

    If we look at conspiracy theories as a belief system, what would this mean for the climate change debate?

    Philosophy, Politics & Society · Supervisor: Carmen Lea Dege

    The problem: debunking and fact-checking keep failing against conspiracy theories, because the beliefs aren't really built on wrong facts. They're rooted in people's culture and worldview. So instead of asking what's false, the thesis asks why people believe.

    PHASE 1 · REDEFINE

    Rejects the standard picture of conspiracy theorists as ill-minded manipulators. Drawing on existential and epistemic motives, it argues conspiracy theories work as a higher-order belief system, offering comfort and reassurance much as religious doctrines do.

    PHASE 2 · CLIMATE

    Climate change is a special case. Accepting the evidence means accepting a dark future, which causes real anxiety. Motivated reasoning then pushes people to reject findings that clash with their politics and social group.

    PHASE 3 · CLIMATEGATE

    The hacked emails from the Climatic Research Unit and the media storm that followed damaged public trust in climate science, making the case for transparency and open discussion about research.

    RESULT › Don't isolate conspiracy believers; engage them. Treating their views as a belief system rather than "bad information" makes the climate debate more productive and more inclusive.

    🧵 The thread to the MSc: one thesis asks why people believe what they do, the other predicts whom they choose.
